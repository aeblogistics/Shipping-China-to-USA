## How Much Does Port-to-Port Shipping Cost?

 Port-to-port ocean freight rates depend on container size, origin port, destination port, and the time of year you book. A 20-foot container moving to the U.S. West Coast typically costs less than the same container routed to the East Coast, where longer routes add transit time and distance.

 Rates also shift with carrier capacity. During peak shipping months, carriers add peak season surcharges to the base ocean freight rate, and these can change the total more than people expect.

 Don’t mistake a port-to-port quote for your full shipping cost. It covers only the ocean leg, so you’ll still need to add origin charges, destination charges, and customs brokerage to calculate your total landed cost.

### What Should a Port-to-Port Freight Quote Include?

 A port-to-port freight quote should spell out the base ocean freight rate, container type, port pair, and any carrier surcharges tied to the sailing. We list these separately instead of rolling them into one number, so you know exactly what the ocean carrier is charging before you book.

 What a port-to-port quote leaves out matters just as much. Factory pickup, export documentation in China, import customs clearance, duties, and inland trucking at destination aren’t part of this scope. If your freight forwarder’s quote already includes those items, you are looking at door-to-door shipping rather than a port-to-port quote.

### Which Charges Can Arise at the Destination Port?

 The U.S. port bills terminal handling charges separately from the ocean freight rate once it unloads your container. Demurrage charges apply if the container stays at the terminal past its free time, while detention charges apply if you keep the carrier’s container or chassis longer than allowed.

 The port and carrier set their own free-time windows, separate from your customs clearance timeline. A customs delay on your end can trigger demurrage even if the ocean freight arrives on schedule.

 We list these as separate charges when we quote a shipment, so you can plan your customs and drayage timing around them instead of discovering them after the container lands.

### Ocean Freight Options: Full Container Load or Less than Container Load

 Full container load (FCL) gives your cargo a dedicated container in 20-foot, 40-foot, and 40-foot high-cube sizes. It suits large shipments or cargo that needs tighter security, since no one opens the container for consolidation along the way.

 LCL shipping fits smaller or test-order volumes that don’t fill a container. We consolidate your cargo with other shipments heading to the same U.S. port, so you pay for the space you use rather than a full container rate. LCL freight requires deconsolidation at a container freight station on arrival, adding a step before your cargo clears customs.

## Who Handles Customs, Documents, and Port Handoffs?

 The importer handles customs clearance on a port-to-port shipment unless you’ve arranged brokerage separately through AEB Logistics. A freight forwarder moves the physical cargo; a licensed customs broker files the entry with U.S. Customs and Border Protection. They’re not the same role.

 Getting the documentation right before departure prevents most of the delays we see at U.S. ports.

### Importer of Record, Customs Broker, and Duty Responsibilities

 The Importer of Record is legally responsible for the accuracy of the customs entry, including classification, value, and duty payment. You need to name a business or individual before the cargo ships, not after it arrives.

 A customs broker can file the entry on the importer’s behalf, but first needs a signed power of attorney from the importer. When requested, we can coordinate [customs brokerage](https://aeblogistics.com/customs-brokerage/) as part of your shipment. This service usually costs around USD 125, depending on the number of commodities on the entry. Note that AEB Logistics does not act as Importer of Record; that responsibility stays with the importing business.

### Documents and Filing Information to Prepare Before Departure

 Every port-to-port shipment needs a matching commercial invoice, packing list, and bill of lading before the cargo loads. Make sure all three documents agree on carton counts, weights, and product descriptions. A mismatch is one of the fastest ways to trigger extra scrutiny at the U.S. port.

 Your HS code, called an HTSUS code once matched to the U.S. tariff schedule, determines the duty rate applied at entry. Many goods from China also carry Section 301 tariffs on top of the standard rate, so both the code and country of origin affect what you’ll owe. Once we have your shipment details, we can provide HS/HTS code guidance and import duty estimation support.

### How FOB, CIF, FAS, and DDP Change the Scope of Service

 Under FOB shipping, your supplier covers origin costs up to loading on the vessel at the China port, and you take over from there. With FAS (Free Alongside Ship), the seller delivers goods alongside the vessel at the port, leaving loading and sea freight to the buyer. FOB pairs naturally with port-to-port bookings.

 CIF (Cost, Insurance, and Freight) means the supplier arranges the ocean freight and insurance, but you still handle customs and delivery at the U.S. end.

 DDP shipping has a different scope entirely. Under DDP (Delivered Duty Paid), the seller or freight forwarder covers duties, taxes, and clearance all the way to a named destination warehouse. Confirm which Incoterm applies to your purchase order before requesting a quote, since it dictates AEB Logistics’ operational scope.
